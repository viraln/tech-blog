## Quantum Logic vs. Classical Logic: A Comparative Analysis

Classical logic, the foundation of modern computers, relies on Boolean algebra, with operations like AND, OR, and NOT.  These operations are deterministic and irreversible.  In contrast, quantum logic, encompassing WFL, utilizes quantum gates that operate on qubits in superposition.  These gates are often reversible, meaning the input can be reconstructed from the output.

| Feature          | Classical Logic                               | Quantum Logic (WFL)                          |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Basic Unit       | Bit (0 or 1)                                 | Qubit (superposition of 0 and 1)             |
| Operations       | AND, OR, NOT (irreversible)                   | Quantum gates (reversible)                     |
| Computation      | Deterministic                                  | Probabilistic                                  |
| Parallelism     | Limited                                       | Exponential (due to superposition)           |
| Information Loss | Present in irreversible operations            | Minimal, ideally reversible                  |

## Reversible Logic Gates: The Heart of Wave Function Logic

Reversible logic gates are crucial for WFL because they preserve information, a critical requirement for minimizing errors in quantum computations.  Unlike irreversible classical gates, reversible gates allow reconstruction of the input from the output.  Examples include the Toffoli gate (a three-bit reversible gate) and the Fredkin gate (a three-bit controlled swap gate). ![Diagrams illustrating Toffoli and Fredkin gates](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1506988038666-496173f39dea?q=85&w=1200&fit=max&fm=webp&auto=compress)

## Wave Function Collapse and Measurement

A crucial aspect of WFL is the concept of wave function collapse.  When a measurement is performed on a quantum system in superposition, the wave function collapses into a single definite state.  This collapse is inherently probabilistic, with the probability of each outcome determined by the wave function before the measurement.  ![Illustration of wave function collapse](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1507963901243-ebfaecd5f2f4?q=85&w=1200&fit=max&fm=webp&auto=compress)
