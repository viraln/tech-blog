## Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR): Scaling Up Atmospheric Carbon Capture Technology

Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) refers to technologies that directly remove CO2 from the atmosphere. It's a critical component of atmosphere engineering because it addresses the root cause of climate change: the excess of greenhouse gases. In the next decade, we can expect to see significant advancements and scaling up of various CDR approaches:

*   **Direct Air Capture (DAC):** DAC plants use chemical processes to capture CO2 directly from the air. While currently expensive, innovation is driving down costs. Expect to see larger-scale DAC facilities deployed, with improved energy efficiency and optimized sorbent materials. By 2035, DAC could become a more economically viable option for atmospheric carbon capture technology.
*   **Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS):** BECCS involves growing biomass for energy, capturing the CO2 emitted during combustion, and storing it underground. The next decade will see increased investment in BECCS projects, with a focus on sustainable biomass sourcing and secure CO2 storage.
*   **Enhanced Weathering:** This approach involves spreading minerals that naturally absorb CO2, such as basalt, on land or in the ocean. Research is focused on optimizing mineral types, application methods, and monitoring the long-term effects. Expect to see pilot projects testing enhanced weathering at scale.
*   **Afforestation and Reforestation:** Planting trees is a natural and cost-effective way to remove CO2. The next decade will see increased efforts to restore forests globally, with a focus on biodiversity and sustainable forest management practices.
*   **Ocean Fertilization:** Adding nutrients to the ocean to stimulate phytoplankton growth, which absorbs CO2, is a controversial CDR method. Research is ongoing to assess the potential benefits and risks of ocean fertilization, but widespread deployment is unlikely in the next decade due to environmental concerns.

> **EXPERT TIP:** The key to successful CDR is scalability, cost-effectiveness, and minimizing unintended environmental consequences. A diversified portfolio of CDR technologies is crucial.

## Solar Radiation Management (SRM): A Controversial But Potentially Necessary Intervention

Solar Radiation Management (SRM) aims to reduce the amount of sunlight absorbed by the Earth. It does not address the underlying cause of climate change but could potentially provide a temporary cooling effect. SRM is highly controversial due to potential risks and uncertainties, but research is ongoing to better understand its potential impacts.

*   **Stratospheric Aerosol Injection (SAI):** This involves injecting aerosols, such as sulfur dioxide, into the stratosphere to reflect sunlight back into space. SAI is the most studied SRM method, but it also raises significant concerns about ozone depletion, regional climate changes, and termination effects (rapid warming if deployment is stopped). Expect to see continued research and modeling studies, but large-scale deployment is unlikely in the next decade without significantly more data and international consensus.
*   **Marine Cloud Brightening (MCB):** MCB involves spraying seawater into low-lying marine clouds to increase their reflectivity. This method is considered less risky than SAI, but its effectiveness and potential regional impacts are still uncertain. Pilot projects are underway to test MCB in specific locations.
*   **Space-Based Reflectors:** Placing large mirrors or reflectors in space to deflect sunlight is a technically challenging and expensive SRM approach. While conceptually interesting, it's unlikely to be feasible in the next decade.

> **DID YOU KNOW?** The eruption of Mount Pinatubo in 1991 injected large amounts of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, causing a temporary global cooling of about 0.5°C. This event provided valuable insights into the potential effects of SAI.

*   **International Governance Frameworks:** Atmosphere engineering raises complex ethical, legal, and political issues that require international cooperation. Expect to see efforts to develop governance frameworks that address issues such as risk assessment, transparency, public engagement, and liability.
