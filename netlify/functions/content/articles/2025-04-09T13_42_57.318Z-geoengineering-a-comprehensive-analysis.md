## What is Geoengineering?

Geoengineering encompasses a broad range of techniques aimed at mitigating climate change.  These methods broadly fall into two categories: solar radiation management (SRM) and carbon dioxide removal (CDR).  SRM techniques focus on reflecting sunlight back into space to cool the planet, while CDR methods aim to actively remove greenhouse gases from the atmosphere.  ![A graphic illustrating the difference between SRM and CDR](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1451187580459-43490279c0fa?q=85&w=1200&fit=max&fm=webp&auto=compress).

> **DID YOU KNOW?** The concept of geoengineering isn't new; proposals for large-scale climate intervention date back to the 1960s, though the urgency has intensified considerably in recent years.

Examples of SRM include stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI), mimicking the cooling effect of volcanic eruptions, and marine cloud brightening (MCB), increasing the reflectivity of clouds.  CDR methods include afforestation (planting trees), bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), direct air capture (DAC), and ocean fertilization.  Each method presents unique challenges and uncertainties.

## Solar Geoengineering: A Closer Look

Solar geoengineering, primarily involving SAI, is the most discussed SRM technique.  The basic premise is to inject reflective particles, such as sulfur dioxide, into the stratosphere, creating a temporary haze that reflects sunlight back into space.  While potentially effective in rapidly cooling the planet, SAI carries significant risks.  These include disruptions to the monsoon cycle, ozone depletion, and unforeseen regional climate impacts.  ![A visualization of stratospheric aerosol injection](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1484417894907-623942c8ee29?q=85&w=1200&fit=max&fm=webp&auto=compress).

> **EXPERT TIP:**  The potential unintended consequences of SAI are a major source of concern, highlighting the need for extensive research and modeling before any large-scale deployment.

Currently, there are no large-scale SAI projects underway, but several research initiatives are exploring its feasibility and potential consequences through computer simulations and small-scale experiments.

## Carbon Capture and Storage: Removing CO2 from the Atmosphere

Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) offers a more direct approach to climate change mitigation.  Unlike SRM, which addresses symptoms, CDR tackles the root cause by removing CO2 from the atmosphere.  Several CDR technologies are being developed, each with varying levels of maturity and scalability.

* 🔑 **Direct Air Capture (DAC):**  This technology uses specialized equipment to directly capture CO2 from ambient air.  While effective, DAC is currently energy-intensive and expensive.
* ⚡ **Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS):** This involves growing biomass, burning it for energy, and capturing the resulting CO2 for storage.  BECCS faces challenges related to land use and potential competition with food production.
* ✅ **Ocean Fertilization:**  This involves adding nutrients to the ocean to stimulate phytoplankton growth, which absorbs CO2.  However, the ecological impacts of ocean fertilization are poorly understood and potentially harmful. ![A diagram showcasing different Carbon Capture methods](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1517976487492-5750f3195933?q=85&w=1200&fit=max&fm=webp&auto=compress).
