## What is Geoengineering?

Geoengineering, also known as climate engineering, refers to large-scale, deliberate interventions in the Earth's climate system to counteract the effects of climate change. These technologies aim to either remove carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere (Carbon Dioxide Removal - CDR) or reduce the amount of sunlight absorbed by the Earth (Solar Radiation Management - SRM). Unlike mitigation, which focuses on reducing emissions, geoengineering attempts to directly manipulate the climate system.

It's crucial to understand that geoengineering is not a replacement for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Most scientists view it as a potential supplement, to be considered only if mitigation efforts fall short of preventing dangerous levels of warming.

## Two Main Approaches: Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) vs. Solar Radiation Management (SRM)

Geoengineering strategies are broadly categorized into two main approaches:

*   **Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR):** These techniques aim to remove CO2 that has already been emitted into the atmosphere. The goal is to reduce the concentration of greenhouse gases and address the root cause of climate change.

*   **Solar Radiation Management (SRM):** These methods focus on reflecting a small fraction of incoming sunlight back into space, thereby cooling the planet. SRM techniques do not address the underlying problem of greenhouse gas emissions and only mask the symptoms of climate change.

> **EXPERT TIP:** Understanding the difference between CDR and SRM is crucial. CDR addresses the cause of climate change, while SRM only addresses the symptoms.

## Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) Technologies: Sucking CO2 from the Air

CDR technologies aim to remove CO2 from the atmosphere and store it permanently. Several promising CDR approaches are currently being researched and developed:

*   **Afforestation and Reforestation:** Planting trees is a natural and relatively low-tech way to absorb CO2. However, large-scale afforestation requires significant land area and can have impacts on biodiversity and water resources.

*   **Direct Air Capture (DAC):** DAC technologies use machines to chemically extract CO2 directly from the air. The captured CO2 can then be stored underground or used to create synthetic fuels or other products. While promising, DAC is currently energy-intensive and expensive.

*   **Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS):** BECCS involves growing biomass (e.g., trees or crops) for energy, capturing the CO2 released during combustion, and storing it underground. BECCS has the potential to be carbon-negative, but it requires careful management to avoid negative impacts on land use and biodiversity.

*   **Enhanced Weathering:** This involves spreading crushed rocks (e.g., basalt) on land or in the ocean to accelerate the natural weathering process, which absorbs CO2. Enhanced weathering is a relatively low-cost option, but it requires large quantities of rock and can have environmental impacts.

*   **Ocean Fertilization:** This involves adding nutrients (e.g., iron) to the ocean to stimulate the growth of phytoplankton, which absorb CO2. Ocean fertilization is controversial due to potential unintended consequences for marine ecosystems.

## Solar Radiation Management (SRM) Technologies: Reflecting Sunlight

SRM technologies aim to reduce the amount of sunlight absorbed by the Earth. The most widely discussed SRM technique is:

*   **Stratospheric Aerosol Injection (SAI):** This involves injecting aerosols (e.g., sulfur dioxide) into the stratosphere to reflect sunlight back into space. SAI is inspired by the cooling effect of volcanic eruptions, which release large amounts of aerosols into the stratosphere.

SAI is potentially the most effective and affordable SRM technique, but it also carries significant risks:

*   **Uneven Cooling:** SAI may not cool the planet evenly, leading to regional climate changes.
*   **Ozone Depletion:** Some aerosols can deplete the ozone layer.
*   **Termination Shock:** If SAI were to be stopped abruptly, the planet would rapidly warm, potentially causing severe ecological disruption.
*   **Altered Precipitation Patterns:** SAI could alter precipitation patterns, leading to droughts in some regions and floods in others.

Other SRM techniques include:

*   **Marine Cloud Brightening:** Spraying seawater into low-lying marine clouds to make them more reflective.
*   **Space-Based Reflectors:** Deploying mirrors or other reflective devices in space to reflect sunlight.

## Geoengineering Pros and Cons: A Complex Equation

Geoengineering presents a complex equation with potential benefits and risks.

**Pros:**

✅ Could rapidly cool the planet in the event of a climate emergency.
✅ Some techniques, like afforestation, offer co-benefits such as improved air quality and biodiversity.
✅ Potentially less expensive than drastic emission reductions in the short term.
✅ Could buy time for the development and deployment of more sustainable solutions.

**Cons:**

❌ Carries significant environmental risks and uncertainties.
❌ Does not address the underlying problem of greenhouse gas emissions.
❌ Could have unintended consequences for regional climates and ecosystems.
❌ Raises ethical and governance challenges.
❌ Potential for termination shock if SRM is stopped abruptly.
❌ Could distract from efforts to reduce emissions.

> **DID YOU KNOW:** The eruption of Mount Pinatubo in 1991 injected enough sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere to cool the planet by about 0.5°C for several years. This event has served as a natural experiment for understanding the potential effects of SAI.

## Is Geoengineering Safe? Assessing the Risks

The safety of geoengineering is a major concern. Many geoengineering techniques carry significant environmental risks, including:

*   **Ozone depletion:** SAI could deplete the ozone layer, increasing the risk of skin cancer and other health problems.
*   **Altered precipitation patterns:** Geoengineering could disrupt rainfall patterns, leading to droughts and floods.
*   **Ocean acidification:** SRM does not address ocean acidification, which is caused by the absorption of CO2 by the ocean.
*   **Unintended consequences for ecosystems:** Geoengineering could have unforeseen impacts on plant and animal life.

> **IMPORTANT NOTE:** The risks of geoengineering are not fully understood. More research is needed to assess the potential consequences of these technologies.
