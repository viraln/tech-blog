## Understanding Dark Energy: The Universe's Biggest Secret

Dark energy, unlike dark matter, isn't a substance we can directly observe. Instead, its existence is inferred from the accelerating expansion of the universe, a phenomenon discovered in the late 1990s. It makes up roughly 68% of the universe's total energy density, dwarfing ordinary matter (like stars and planets) and even dark matter.

The most widely accepted model for dark energy is the cosmological constant, a term introduced by Einstein in his theory of general relativity. This constant represents a uniform energy density that permeates all of space and doesn't dilute as the universe expands. However, the observed value of the cosmological constant is vastly smaller than theoretical predictions, leading to the "cosmological constant problem," one of the biggest challenges in modern physics.

Other models propose that dark energy is a dynamic field, dubbed "quintessence," which changes over time and space. These models attempt to address the cosmological constant problem by suggesting that the energy density of dark energy can vary, potentially even becoming negative in the future.
