## 1. Expanding Legal Standing: From Property to Personhood

For centuries, animals have been legally classified as property, devoid of inherent rights. This classification allows for their exploitation and often overlooks their well-being. Multispecies law challenges this archaic view by advocating for legal standing for animals – the ability to bring a case before a court.

This isn’t about granting animals the right to vote or hold office. Instead, it focuses on ensuring that their interests are considered in legal proceedings that directly impact them. The core argument rests on the premise that sentience, the capacity to experience feelings and sensations, should be the basis for legal consideration.

> **EXPERT TIP:** Legal standing doesn't require full human rights. It simply means that an entity has the right to be heard in court and have its interests considered.

## 2. The Rights of Nature: Protecting Ecosystems as Legal Entities

Beyond individual animal rights, multispecies law is also exploring the concept of the Rights of Nature. This radical idea proposes that ecosystems – rivers, forests, mountains – should be recognized as legal entities with inherent rights to exist, flourish, and evolve.

This approach shifts the focus from viewing nature as a resource to be exploited to recognizing its intrinsic value and ecological importance. It acknowledges that healthy ecosystems are essential for human well-being and that their destruction has far-reaching consequences.

Several countries and local jurisdictions have already adopted Rights of Nature laws. Ecuador, for example, enshrined the Rights of Nature in its constitution in 2008. Rivers in New Zealand and India have also been granted legal personhood, recognizing their spiritual and cultural significance and providing them with legal protection.

## 3. Integrating Animal Welfare into Environmental Law: A Holistic Approach

Traditionally, animal rights law and environmental law have operated as separate fields. Animal rights law focuses primarily on the treatment of individual animals, while environmental law focuses on protecting ecosystems and biodiversity. Multispecies law seeks to bridge this gap by integrating animal welfare considerations into environmental protection efforts.

This means recognizing that the well-being of individual animals is inextricably linked to the health of their ecosystems. For example, protecting endangered species requires not only preventing poaching and habitat destruction but also ensuring that animals have access to adequate food, water, and shelter.

**Example: The Case of the Vaquita**

The Vaquita, the world's smallest porpoise, is critically endangered, with only a handful remaining in the Gulf of California. The primary threat to their survival is entanglement in gillnets used to catch shrimp and totoaba, a fish whose swim bladder is highly valued in Chinese medicine.

Traditional conservation efforts have focused on banning gillnets and cracking down on illegal fishing. However, a multispecies law approach would also consider the economic and social needs of the local fishing communities, providing them with alternative livelihoods and ensuring that they have a stake in the Vaquita's survival. This integrated approach recognizes that protecting the Vaquita requires addressing the root causes of the problem and fostering a sense of shared responsibility.
